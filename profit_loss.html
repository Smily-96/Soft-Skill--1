Profit & Loss

Cost Price and Selling Price

▼
Cost Price (C.P.) Definition

The price at which an article is bought by a trader is called its cost price, written as C.P. This is the amount actually spent to acquire the goods, before any expenses of resale.

A trader buys a chair for ₹600 → C.P. of the chair = ₹600
Selling Price (S.P.) Definition

The price at which an article is sold is called its selling price, written as S.P. Comparing S.P. with C.P. tells us whether the trader made a profit or suffered a loss.

The same chair is sold for ₹765 → S.P. of the chair = ₹765
📝 Note: C.P. and S.P. must refer to the same article and the same quantity — mixing units (e.g. C.P. per dozen with S.P. per piece) is the most common source of error in this topic.
Ex: Mansi purchased a car for ₹2,50,000 and sold it for ₹3,48,000. State the C.P. and S.P.
  1. The amount Mansi paid to buy the car is the cost price.
  2. C.P. = ₹2,50,000.
  3. The amount she received when she sold it is the selling price.
  4. Answer: C.P. = ₹2,50,000, S.P. = ₹3,48,000
📈

Profit (Gain)

▼
Gain / Profit Definition

If the selling price is greater than the cost price, the seller is said to make a profit or gain. The gain is always measured in relation to the cost price, never the selling price.

Gain = S.P. − C.P. (only when S.P. > C.P.)
  • Gain is always reckoned on C.P. — "gain of 20%" always means 20% of the cost price, unless a question explicitly says otherwise.
  • Gain % formula: Gain % = (Gain × 100) ⁄ C.P.
  • If an article is sold at a gain of x%, then S.P. = (100 + x)% of C.P.
Ex: A gold bracelet costing ₹14,500 is bought and later sold for ₹18,125. Find the gain and gain%.
  1. C.P. = ₹14,500 and S.P. = ₹18,125. Since S.P. > C.P., there is a gain.
  2. Gain = S.P. − C.P. = 18,125 − 14,500 = ₹3,625.
  3. Gain % = (Gain × 100) ⁄ C.P. = (3625 × 100) ⁄ 14500 = 25%.
  4. Answer: Gain = ₹3,625, Gain% = 25%
📉

Loss

▼
Loss Definition

If the selling price is less than the cost price, the seller is said to have incurred a loss. Like gain, loss is also always calculated as a percentage of the cost price.

Loss = C.P. − S.P. (only when C.P. > S.P.)
  • Loss % formula: Loss % = (Loss × 100) ⁄ C.P.
  • If an article is sold at a loss of x%, then S.P. = (100 − x)% of C.P.
  • To recover a loss on a fresh sale, the new selling price must be found by working from the original C.P., not from the previous (lower) S.P.
Ex: A shopkeeper buys a book for ₹2,516 and sells it for ₹2,272. Find the loss and loss%.
  1. C.P. = ₹2,516, S.P. = ₹2,272. Since C.P. > S.P., there is a loss.
  2. Loss = C.P. − S.P. = 2516 − 2272 = ₹244.
  3. Loss % = (Loss × 100) ⁄ C.P. = (244 × 100) ⁄ 2516 ≈ 9.7%.
  4. Answer: Loss = ₹244, Loss% ≈ 9.7%
🔗

Key Relationships & Formulas

▼
Gain / Loss
Gain = S.P. − C.P.
Loss = C.P. − S.P.
Gain % / Loss %
Gain% = (Gain×100)/C.P.
Loss% = (Loss×100)/C.P.
S.P. from C.P.
S.P. = (100+Gain%)/100 × C.P.
S.P. = (100−Loss%)/100 × C.P.
C.P. from S.P.
C.P. = 100/(100+Gain%) × S.P.
C.P. = 100/(100−Loss%) × S.P.
Reading Percentages of C.P. Shortcut

If an article is sold at a gain of 35%, S.P. is simply 135% of C.P. If it is sold at a loss of 35%, S.P. is 65% of C.P. — this saves re-deriving the formula every time.

Gain of 35% → S.P. = 135% of C.P. | Loss of 35% → S.P. = 65% of C.P.
Ex: Find the S.P. when C.P. = ₹56.25 and gain = 20%.
  1. Since the gain is 20%, S.P. = 120% of C.P.
  2. S.P. = (120/100) × 56.25.
  3. S.P. = 67.50.
  4. Answer: S.P. = ₹67.50

Special Case: Equal Percentage Gain and Loss

▼
Two Articles, Same x%, Opposite Outcome Rule

When a person sells two similar items, one at a gain of x% and the other at a loss of the same x%, the overall result on the whole transaction is always a loss — never a break-even, no matter what x is.

Common Loss % = (x/10)²
  • This happens because both articles are sold for the same price, but their cost prices are unequal — the article sold at a loss actually cost more to buy back at that discounted percentage.
  • The result is always a percentage loss, computed on the sum of the two cost prices — it is never a gain.
Ex: A man sells two horses for ₹1,475 each, one at 10% gain, the other at 10% loss overall. What is the net result?
  1. Using the rule, common loss % = (x/10)² where x = 10.
  2. Common loss % = (10/10)² = 1².
  3. Common loss % = 1%.
  4. Answer: Overall the trader incurs a loss of 1%

Direct Formula Method

▼
  • Step 1: Identify what is given — C.P., S.P., gain/loss amount, or gain/loss %.
  • Step 2: Pick the matching formula: Gain%=(Gain×100)/C.P., or S.P.=(100±Gain/Loss%)/100 × C.P., or C.P.=100/(100±Gain/Loss%) × S.P.
  • Step 3: Substitute the known values directly and solve for the unknown.
💡 Best for: straightforward one-article problems where two of C.P., S.P., and Gain%/Loss% are already known.
Ex: The cost price of an article is ₹7,840. Find the selling price so that there is a gain of 7.5%.
  1. Use S.P. = (100 + Gain%)/100 × C.P.
  2. S.P. = (107.5/100) × 7840.
  3. S.P. = 1.075 × 7840 = 8428.
  4. Answer: S.P. = ₹8,428
Ex: By selling an umbrella for ₹300, a shopkeeper gains 20%. Find the cost price.
  1. Use C.P. = 100/(100 + Gain%) × S.P.
  2. C.P. = (100/120) × 300.
  3. C.P. = 250.
  4. Answer: C.P. = ₹250
⚖️

Ratio (Unit C.P.) Method

▼
  • Step 1: Assume the cost price of one unit (one article, one metre, one kg) to be ₹1, or take the L.C.M. of the two quantities mentioned in the question.
  • Step 2: Write C.P. and S.P. of the full stated batch purely in these unit terms.
  • Step 3: Compute Gain or Loss directly on this convenient batch size, then convert to a percentage.
💡 Best for: problems phrased as "C.P. of x articles equals S.P. of y articles" — a very common exam pattern that avoids messy algebra.
Ex: If the C.P. of 15 books equals the S.P. of 20 books, find the loss percent.
  1. Let the C.P. of each book be ₹1.
  2. Then C.P. of 20 books = ₹20, and S.P. of 20 books = C.P. of 15 books = ₹15.
  3. Loss = 20 − 15 = ₹5 on a C.P. of ₹20.
  4. Answer: Loss% = (5×100)/20 = 25%
🔁

Successive Percentage Change Method

▼
  • Step 1: Represent every markup or discount as a multiplying factor: a gain/markup of x% multiplies by (100+x)/100, a loss/discount of x% multiplies by (100−x)/100.
  • Step 2: Chain the multiplying factors together in the order the changes happen.
  • Step 3: Compare the final multiplying factor to 1 (i.e. 100%) to read off the overall gain or loss percentage.
💡 Best for: marked-price-then-discount problems, and any chain of two or more successive percentage changes.
Ex: The marked price of an article is 30% above its cost price. After two successive discounts of 10% each, find the overall effect on the price.
  1. Let C.P. = 100. Marked price = 130% of 100 = 130.
  2. Apply the first 10% discount: 130 × 90/100 = 117.
  3. Apply the second 10% discount: 117 × 90/100 = 105.3.
  4. Answer: Final price = 105.3, an overall increase of 5.3% over C.P.

Marked Price & Discount Problems

▼
When you see "marked" and "discount" Pattern

Whenever a question gives a marked (list) price and a discount %, the actual selling price is Marked Price minus the discount amount. Compare this S.P. to the C.P. to find gain or loss — never assume marked price equals cost price.

Ex: An article listed at ₹900 is given two successive discounts of 8% and 8%. Find the equivalent single discount, and compare it with a straight 16% discount.
  1. Price after first 8% discount: 900 × 92/100 = 828.
  2. Price after second 8% discount: 828 × 92/100 = 761.76.
  3. A single 16% discount would give: 900 × 84/100 = 756.
  4. Answer: Two successive 8% discounts (₹761.76) leave the seller ₹5.76 better off than one 16% discount (₹756)
Ex: A shopkeeper marks goods 40% above cost price and allows a discount of 10%. Find the gain percent.
  1. Let C.P. = 100. Marked price = 140% of 100 = 140.
  2. S.P. after 10% discount = 90% of 140 = 126.
  3. Gain = 126 − 100 = 26 on a C.P. of 100.
  4. Answer: Gain% = 26%
⚖️

False Weight / Cheating Problems

▼
Pattern Rule

Whenever a dealer "professes to sell at cost price" but secretly uses a weight lighter than what is stated, the dealer still makes a gain. Treat the true weight given as the actual quantity supplied, and the stated weight (e.g. 1 kg) as what the customer pays for.

Gain% = [Error / (True Value − Error)] × 100
Ex: A dealer professes to sell his goods at cost price but uses a false weight of 950 grams for a kilogram. Find his gain percent.
  1. He gives only 950 g but charges for 1000 g, so the error is 1000−950=50 g.
  2. Gain% = [Error / (True value − Error)] × 100 = [50 / (1000−50)] × 100.
  3. Gain% = 5000/950 ≈ 5.26%.
  4. Answer: Gain% ≈ 5 5⁄19 %
Ex: A fruit seller professes to sell his fruits at cost price, but still gains 25% on his outlay. What weight does he substitute for a kilogram?
  1. Let the error (shortfall) be x grams out of 1000 g.
  2. Using Gain% = [x/(1000−x)]×100 = 25, so 100x = 25(1000−x).
  3. 100x = 25000 − 25x → 125x = 25000 → x = 200.
  4. Answer: He actually gives only 800 g for every claimed kilogram
🔁

Successive Discounts

▼
Pattern Rule

Whenever a question describes two or more discounts applied one after another, multiply the successive "remaining fraction" factors together rather than adding the percentages — successive discounts of p% and q% are never equivalent to a single (p+q)% discount.

Single equivalent discount % = 100 − [(100−p)(100−q)/100]
Ex: Find a single discount equivalent to two successive discounts of 10% and 12%.
  1. Let the marked price be 100.
  2. After 10% discount: 100 × 90/100 = 90. After 12% discount: 90 × 88/100 = 79.2.
  3. The final price is 79.2, so the price has fallen by 100 − 79.2 = 20.8.
  4. Answer: Single equivalent discount = 20.8%
🧺

Mixed Goods Sold at Different Rates

▼
Pattern Rule

When part of a stock is sold at one gain/loss % and the remaining part at another, find the total C.P. and total S.P. of the whole stock separately, then compute the overall gain or loss % from those two totals — never simply average the two percentages.

Ex: A man buys goods worth ₹6,000 and sells half of them at a gain of 10%. At what gain % must he sell the remainder to get an overall gain of 25% on the whole?
  1. Total C.P. = ₹6,000. Desired total S.P. for 25% overall gain = 125% of 6000 = ₹7,500.
  2. S.P. of the first half (C.P. ₹3,000) at 10% gain = 110% of 3000 = ₹3,300.
  3. Required S.P. of the second half = 7500 − 3300 = ₹4,200, on a C.P. of ₹3,000.
  4. Answer: Gain% on remainder = (1200/3000)×100 = 40%
🔄

Same Selling Price, Opposite Gain/Loss %

▼
💡 Common setup: "Two articles are sold at the same price, one at a gain of x%, the other at a loss of y%." Find each article's C.P. separately from the shared S.P., then combine to see the overall result — the two percentages must never be averaged directly.
Ex: A man sells two articles for ₹99 each. He gains 10% on one and loses 10% on the other. Find his overall gain or loss.
  1. C.P. of first article = 100/110 × 99 = 90. C.P. of second article = 100/90 × 99 = 110.
  2. Total C.P. = 90 + 110 = 200. Total S.P. = 99 + 99 = 198.
  3. Since total S.P. (198) is less than total C.P. (200), there is an overall loss of 200−198=2.
  4. Answer: Overall loss = ₹2 (this matches the (x/10)² special-case rule: (10/10)²=1% of 200=₹2)
